# FPL Team Building

Reference document on constructing an FPL squad: squad composition, formations, premium allocation, price-point targeting, budget players, bench strategy, and structure planning.

## Squad composition

- Every squad has 15 players: 2 goalkeepers, 5 defenders, 5 midfielders, 3 forwards.
- Each Gameweek: starting XI + 4 substitutes (1 GK + 3 outfield, in set order).
- Team structure = orientation of the squad by positions, prices and formation. Structure matters because it determines flexibility, hit exposure, routes to high-ownership players, and money in the bank.
- A fixed structure helps season planning; aim to know why the team is built this way, not just what it is.

## Formations

- Most common: 3-4-3 and 3-5-2. They balance attacking cover with a playable bench.
- Viable alternatives depend on season pricing: 4-4-2, 4-3-3, and (in defensive-contribution eras) 4-5-1 or 5-4-1.
- Formation should be flexible week to week; rotating the 7th attacker with the 4th/5th defender is an established technique.
- Rule of thumb: formations with 3 forwards are usually optimal when cheap forwards are scarce; lean on midfield/defence when those positions offer better value.

## Premium allocation

- Standard: 2–3 premiums (£10m+) form the squad's foundation. Premiums provide ceiling and captaincy anchor; the rest of the squad funds them.
- **1 premium (e.g. Haaland only)**: allows a stacked midfield of £7.5–9.0m assets and a strong bench; flexible, good money in the bank. Downside: harder route to a second premium later.
- **2 premiums**: the balanced default; covers most price points with a decent bench. Salah-over-Kane-style choices hinge on which positions offer cheap alternatives.
- **3 premiums (threemium)**: highest ceiling, but eats funds — no £8–9m midfielders, very weak bench, and poor routes to mid-price value when the meta shifts. Rarely sustainable long-term.
- **0 premiums / no Haaland**: viable only as an explicit bet; the risk is not early points but the difficulty of bringing the premium back in (price rises, multi-hits or an early wildcard). Better to start safe, take risks later.

## Price-point targeting (general principles)

Price points change each season; the method is to classify each price band and target the ones with proven value.

- **Goalkeepers**: 4.0 = bench fodder; 4.5–5.0 = target starters; 5.5+ = avoid unless structure demands (GK points are capped; cheap starters offer better value).
- **Defenders**: 4.0 = fodder; 4.5 = value starters from solid defences; 5.0–5.5 = attacking defenders from top teams (clean sheets + attacking returns); 6.0+ = premium defenders, only if the budget allows and clean-sheet probability justifies.
- **Midfielders**: 4.5 = fodder; 5.0–6.5 = value enablers; 7.5–9.0 = the sweet spot (most proven assets live here); 10+ = premium tier.
- **Forwards**: historically the weakest value position (points-per-million); cheap forwards underperform cheap mids/defs because mids add clean-sheet and assist value. 7.0–8.5 mid-price forwards are the target band; 10+ for the elite talisman. Forwards are explosive but inconsistent — expect 2–5 point games with haul upside.
- **Core principle**: midfielders generally outscore forwards per million because of clean-sheet and assist scoring; spend where the rules reward points, not on position labels.

## Transfer flexibility and price headroom

Build routes between price points, not just an attractive starting fifteen. The money available for one replacement is the outgoing player's personal selling price plus the bank, not their public market price or the squad's headline value. If the personal selling price is unknown, a future transfer route is conditional, not a verified budget calculation.

A deliberate bank buffer can keep an intended move affordable after a £0.1m rise or a sale-price fall; choose its size against likely routes and the points forgone by leaving it unspent, not a mandatory fixed amount. When a move is close to the limit, check dated official Price Changes evidence and weigh earlier action against waiting for team news. A historical rise alone is not a forecast, and a useful player should not be sold only to protect the displayed squad value.

See FPL Price Changes and Team Value for profit rounding and realisable funds. Higher market value can improve flexibility, but not all of a gain is spendable or accessible without selling players worth keeping.

## Budget players and enablers

- Every squad needs 2–3 budget enablers (≤£5.5m) to fund premiums. The maths of owning Salah/Haaland + a full squad requires them.
- A good enabler: guaranteed starter, route to attacking/clean-sheet points (even 1–2 returns/month), or a defensively solid team for cheap defenders.
- Minimum bench fodder (4.0 GK, 4.0–4.5 DEF) is acceptable in the cheapest slots, but avoid filling the bench with non-players beyond that — dead slots cost points through the season.
- Do not overspend on the bench: bench points normally do not count unless auto-subs occur, so bench investment pays only in rotation-heavy weeks or with Bench Boost.

## Bench strategy

- Bench order matters: FPL auto-subs in set order, but only for starters who record 0 minutes, and only if the resulting formation stays legal (min 3 DEF, 2 MID, 1 FWD).
- A one-minute cameo locks the starter in and blocks the bench replacement — late team news and rotation risk are the main threats.
- Two philosophies: (a) cheap bench (fodder, save funds for XI) vs (b) playing bench (reliable minutes for rotation and injury cover). Choose by risk tolerance and chip plans; Bench Boost requires a strong, playing bench.

## Template vs differentials

- The template (most-owned players) is consensus-correct at the premium tier: 74%-owned Haaland-type picks are near-mandatory because their underlying output backs the ownership.
- Ownership cliff: below ~20–25% ownership you reach differentials; this is where separation is created.
- Winning balance: roughly 70–80% template overlap + 20–30% targeted differentials at lower-owned tiers. Pure template finishes mid-rank; pure contrarian finishes bottom.
- Avoid forced contrarianism: 10% ownership alone is not a reason to pick someone; the template exists because top-owned players genuinely deliver.

## Season-long structure planning

- GW1–5: build around opening fixtures; easy early runs multiply value; plan the first wildcard window (~GW5–8) before the season starts, not reactively.
- Monitor fixture swings 4–6 Gameweeks ahead; structure changes should anticipate swings, not chase form.
- Second-half planning: blank/double Gameweek windows (GW25–37) drive wildcard, bench boost and transfer planning; keep structure flexible until schedules are confirmed.
- Structure is not static: early-season data is noisy, so avoid rebuilding on small samples; change structure when information (form, roles, injuries, emerging value) justifies it.
