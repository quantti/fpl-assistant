# Blank and Double Gameweek Planning

Reference document on the fixture-schedule events that decide most chip strategy: what blank and double gameweeks are, when they appear, how far ahead they can be known, and how each chip interacts with them. Written for 2026/27, where each chip comes in two sets — one usable in GW1–19, one in GW20–38 — so a chip saved for a double must be saved inside its own half.

## What they are, and why they exist

- A **blank gameweek (BGW)** is a gameweek in which a club has no fixture, so its players score nothing. Squads carrying several affected clubs can be left with fewer than eleven starters.
- A **double gameweek (DGW)** is a gameweek in which a club plays twice. Its players get two chances at points from one squad slot — the single most exploitable event in the FPL calendar.
- Both come from the same cause: **domestic cup progress and European fixture congestion.** When an FA Cup round or an EFL Cup final lands on a Premier League weekend, the clubs involved have their league fixture postponed — that is the blank. The postponed match is later rescheduled into a midweek slot, and if that slot falls in a week where the club already plays, that is the double.
- Consequence worth internalising: **blanks and doubles are the same fixtures, seen at two different times.** A club that blanks in GW28 is a club with a double later, and the clubs most likely to double are the ones going deepest in cups — usually the strongest squads.

## When they happen

- Blanks cluster around the FA Cup fifth round and quarter-finals, and around the EFL Cup final — in practice the GW26–GW34 range in a typical season, with the exact gameweeks moving every year.
- Doubles cluster in the second half of the season once postponed matches are rescheduled, most often in the GW30s, with a common final-weeks double as the calendar is cleared before the last round.
- The most reliable early signal is not a fixture list at all: it is **which clubs are still in which cups**. A club knocked out of the FA Cup in January is a club that will not blank in the spring.
- The Premier League confirms rescheduled dates only a few weeks ahead, so a "confirmed" double is usually confirmed with two to four gameweeks' notice. Plans built on doubles more than a month out are provisional by nature — say so when advising on them.

## What this means for chips

- **Bench Boost wants a double, and needs fifteen playing bodies.** The chip scores your bench, so its value is the four bench players' points; playing it in a double where several of your fifteen play twice is where the ceiling is. It fails when the bench holds non-playing enablers, so a Bench Boost is planned with a *wildcard one or two weeks earlier* that fills the bench with starters.
- **Triple Captain wants a double at a premium.** Two fixtures for a captain who is already the best pick means the chip doubles a doubled return. A single-gameweek Triple Captain is defensible only when the fixture is exceptional and no double is reachable inside the chip's half.
- **Free Hit is the blank-gameweek answer.** It fields a one-week squad and reverts afterwards, so it turns a gameweek where you have six starters into a full eleven without wrecking the squad you spent months building. It is also the classic way to attack a double without committing transfers, but the blank use is the one that saves a season.
- **Wildcard is the setup chip.** Used one or two gameweeks before a blank or double, it restructures the squad toward the clubs that play — and, crucially, it is the chip that makes the *other* chips work. A Bench Boost without a preceding wildcard is usually a wasted chip.
- **The two-set rule changes the arithmetic.** With one set of chips per half of the season, a chip is not "saved for the run-in" any more if it belongs to the first half. A first-half chip unplayed by GW19 is lost, so the second-half chips are the ones that get spent on blanks and doubles, and the first-half set should be planned against the fixtures that exist before GW19.

## Planning a blank

- Start from the squad, not the calendar: count how many of your fifteen actually have a fixture. Fewer than eleven starters is a Free Hit case; nine or ten with a strong bench may be survivable with one transfer and an accepted low score.
- Transfers made *into* the blank should target clubs that play — not the best players available. A 4-point contribution from a playing defender beats a premium who is not on the pitch.
- Watch the bench order and the vice-captain more carefully than usual: in a blank, autosubs are doing real work, and a captain with no fixture hands the armband to the vice.

## Planning a double

- Rank double-gameweek targets by *minutes certainty first*: a rotated forward with two fixtures may play 90 minutes in total, while a nailed midfielder plays 180. Cup congestion is exactly when the strongest squads rotate hardest.
- Prefer doubles at clubs whose two opponents are both weak. Two hard fixtures is not automatically better than one easy one.
- A double is the best week to captain, and often the best week to take a hit — the hit is paid once and the incoming player plays twice.
- Do not sell a doubling player the week *after* the double on autopilot; the fixture swing usually normalises rather than collapsing.

## Common mistakes

- **Planning a double that never gets confirmed.** Fixtures are rearranged, not promised. Chip plans should have a fallback gameweek.
- **Bench Boost on a squad of enablers.** The chip pays what the bench is worth, and four £4.0m non-starters are worth nearly nothing.
- **Holding a first-half chip into the second half.** It does not carry over; it expires.
- **Ignoring the blank because the double is exciting.** The clubs that double are the clubs that blanked — the same squad decision has to survive both weeks.
- **Chasing a double with multiple hits.** Two hits (-8) demands a large double return before the chip has earned anything.
