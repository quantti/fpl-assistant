# FPL Pre-Season Team Building

Reference document on building an FPL squad from scratch before Gameweek 1: what pre-season is for, the information to trust, structure-first squad construction, template positioning, and the final-week checklist.

## What pre-season is for

- The goal of pre-season is a Gameweek 1 squad, not a finished season squad. GW1 teams are provisional: ownership, prices, roles and fixtures all move in the opening weeks, and the first wildcard is planned rather than reacted to.
- Building from zero means making explicit choices about structure — formation, premium count, budget split, bench — before choosing names. Players come and go; structure is the skeleton that survives transfers.
- Pre-season rewards process over prediction. Managers who do well know what information matters at each stage and refuse to rebuild on noise.

## The pre-season calendar

- Fixtures are released in mid-June; prices are revealed in late June and early July (usually in waves); the game goes live shortly after.
- Friendlies run through July and August. The most useful games are in the final two weeks before the deadline, when first-choice line-ups appear and minutes totals become meaningful.
- The Community Shield (typically mid-August, about a week before GW1) is the first competitive line-up signal for the two finalists.
- The GW1 deadline is usually a Friday evening in late August, roughly half an hour before the opening fixture kicks off. All team news, injuries and last-minute moves must be finalised by then.
- Prices and ownership move daily from reveal to deadline. Anything read more than a few days before the deadline is a snapshot, not a guarantee.

## Structure before names

- Decide the framework first: formation (3-4-3 and 3-5-2 are the defaults), how many premiums, how much money goes to each line, and what the bench looks like.
- Structure determines flexibility: which price points allow a single-transfer move, how quickly the squad can reach a player the meta moves towards, and how much a bad GW1 costs.
- A common opening structure: a cheap but starting goalkeeper pair, four or five defenders who all start (at least two at £4.5m or below), a mid-priced midfield spine with one or two premium midfielders, and a forward line of one premium plus one or two value forwards.
- Leave money in the bank if unsure: £0.5–2.0m of headroom absorbs the first price rise, a pre-season injury, or a structure change without taking a hit.

## Template as the baseline

- The template is the cluster of high-ownership players the community converges on. Owning it protects rank when the popular captain scores; differentiating away from it is where leagues are won.
- A sensible opening squad carries roughly 60–80% template overlap plus one or two genuine differentials (sub-10% ownership) chosen because their expected points are competitive — not just because they are unpopular.
- Pure template finishes mid-rank; pure contrarianism bottoms out. The template exists because the most-owned players are usually the ones with the strongest underlying case.
- Consensus is strongest at the premium tier and weakest at the budget tier — the budget tier is where the separation is made.

## Price-point planning and enablers

- Every GW1 squad needs two or three budget enablers (typically one £4.0–4.5m player in each of two lines) to fund the premiums. A good enabler starts, has a route to points (set pieces, penalties, attacking full-back, solid defence), or is at minimum a nailed starter.
- Know the price points that open one-transfer moves: for example a £4.5m defender to £5.0–5.5m, a £6.0m midfielder to £7.5m, a £7.5m forward to £8.0m. Draft with those gaps in mind.
- Bench policy: two philosophies — fodder bench (cheap, funds the XI) versus playing bench (rotation and injury cover, Bench Boost readiness). Most seasons start with a cheap bench and upgrade it for the Bench Boost window.
- Avoid more than the minimum number of dead slots. Every non-playing bench player is a wasted slot across 38 gameweeks, even if a £4.0m defender saves money now.

## What pre-season information to trust

- Trust: minutes in the last two weeks of friendlies (a minutes tracker is the single most useful pre-season stat), confirmed roles and set-piece duties, penalty takers, first-choice line-ups in the final friendly and the Community Shield, and injury news.
- Ignore: friendly scorelines, individual friendly goals, hype from a single performance, and anything from early friendlies where teams play rotated sides against weak opposition.
- A player returning late from a summer international tournament is a minutes risk regardless of pre-season form; treat them as wait-and-see until their club confirms involvement.
- Position changes and reclassifications are announced with the price reveals — check them before drafting around old assumptions.

## Fixture-led early planning

- Plan the first 4–6 gameweeks, not just GW1: identify the teams with easy opening runs and buy into them, note which picks were bought for fixtures and will need an exit, and know the fixture swings arriving in the first quarter of the season.
- The first wildcard window (roughly GW6–9 in a normal season) should be planned before GW1: know what would trigger it and roughly which players it would target.
- Do not book transfers before the deadline. Pre-season information is cheap and a single friendly injury can invalidate a two-transfer plan.

## Risk management in the GW1 draft

- Start with maximum flexibility and minimum risk: no booked transfers, players at key price points, and at most one genuinely risky pick (not nailed, new to the league, injury-prone) that you are prepared to ship early.
- Do not over-commit to unknowns: new signings, players new to the league, and players with uncertain minutes should be watched rather than owned at GW1, unless their price point makes them disposable.
- Small-sample pre-season data is noise: a defender scoring friendly goals matters less than whether they started the last two games.
- Have a plan B for every pick at the deadline: if they blank in GW1, who replaces them with one transfer?

## Final-week checklist

- Confirm the GW1 deadline and the opening kick-off time.
- Check the last round of friendlies for injuries and first-choice line-ups (minutes tracker, club sites, injury aggregators).
- Check set-piece and penalty duties where uncertain — pre-season spot-kick misses rarely change the taker.
- Check Community Shield line-ups and any cup or European commitments affecting GW1 minutes.
- Re-check prices and ownership if the draft is more than a few days old.
- Set the bench order — auto-subs only replace starters who record zero minutes, and a one-minute cameo blocks the bench.
- Decide the captain and vice-captain with the opening fixtures in mind.
